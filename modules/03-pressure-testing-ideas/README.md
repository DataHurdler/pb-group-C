# Module 03 — Pressure-Testing Ideas

Status: draft
Time: 12:50–14:05 (75 min — lecture 15 / activity 60)
Objective: LO3

## Objective

By the end of this module, participants will be able to **apply** ideation,
simulated-customer, and pre-mortem prompts to their own idea, and **verify** at
least two factual claims the model makes.

## Why this module is here

This is where the day turns to the participant's own work. Innovators mostly use
AI for three things: generating options, imagining how others will react, and
finding holes in a plan. Each has a specific prompt pattern and a specific
trap. Running them as a **chain** in one conversation also teaches
decomposition without a lecture on it. It's the first block after lunch, so it's
the most hands-on block of the day.

### The five moves

| Move | What it's for | The prompt pattern | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ground** | Make sure it understood you | Paste a brief; "confirm in three bullets, no advice yet" | Skipping it, so every later answer rests on a misreading |
| **Diverge** | Get genuinely different options | Specify dimensions to vary; require some "wrong-seeming" ones; ask for a table | Asking for "more creative ideas" gets the same idea in different words |
| **Simulate** | Anticipate objections | Three distinct personas, including why they wouldn't buy | **Treating it as customer research.** It's a list of questions for real customers |
| **Pre-mortem** | Find the holes | "It's 18 months later and this failed. Why?" Ranked, with warning signs | Accepting the first reason; ask for ranked and specific |
| **Verify** | Know what to trust | "List every factual claim; label known / likely / guess", then check the important ones | Trusting the labels. Models are often overconfident about their own claims |

**Why a chain:** each step is small enough to check, and the conversation
builds context as it goes. If one step goes wrong, you fix that step, not the
whole thing.

## Key points

1. Break big asks into a chain of small, checkable steps.
2. To get variety, name *what* should vary.
3. Simulated customers give you questions, not answers.
4. A pre-mortem finds holes faster than asking "what do you think?"
5. Verify the claims your decision rests on, every time.

## Common problems and what to do

| Problem | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Hits a free-tier message cap mid-chain | New chat, paste the step 1 brief, continue. Point out that this is exactly why the brief is worth writing well |
| "The customers loved my idea!" | Say to the whole room, once: the model is generating plausible reactions, and it tends to be agreeable. What would real customers say? |
| Participant is discouraged by the pre-mortem | Reframe: every reason it produced is one you can now check cheaply, before it costs anything |
| Someone pastes something sensitive | Quietly suggest a general description instead; repeat the data rule to the room without naming anyone |
