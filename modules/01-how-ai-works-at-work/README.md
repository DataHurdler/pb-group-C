# Module 01 — How AI Works, and Where It Fits at Work

Status: draft
Time: 9:20–10:20 (60 min — lecture 20 / activity 40)
Objective: LO1

## Objective

By the end of this module, participants will be able to **explain** why an AI
assistant produced a given output, in terms of prediction and context;
**flag** claims that are likely invented; and **sort** workplace tasks into
good fit / use with care / not for AI.

## Why this module is here

Most participants have used an AI assistant and been disappointed by generic or
wrong answers. They usually blame the tool or themselves. This module gives
them a working mental model, just enough to predict what will help and where
the risks are, and a first sense of which work to hand to AI at all.
Everything after it follows: Module 02 because the model only knows what's in
the context, Modules 03–04 because "where it fits" is the business question.

### The four ideas

1. **Prediction, not lookup.** The model writes the most plausible
   continuation of your text, given everything it learned in training. It isn't
   retrieving a stored answer, so the same question can produce different
   answers, and plausible isn't the same as true.
2. **Context is its working memory.** The model knows only what was in its
   training and what's in *this* conversation. It doesn't know your business,
   your customers, or your constraints unless you say so. A generic question
   gets a generic answer.
3. **Confident wrongness.** When it doesn't know, it often produces something
   that *looks* like knowledge: a precise figure, a named source, a competitor.
   Tone tells you nothing about accuracy. Numbers, names, quotes, and recent
   events are the highest-risk claims. Tools that search the web help, but only
   if the sources are about *your* question.
4. **Where it fits at work.** AI is strong at drafting, summarizing,
   reformatting, brainstorming, and first-pass analysis. It's weak at facts it
   wasn't given, exact calculations without checking, and judgments about
   people. Two questions decide most cases: *how bad is it if this is wrong?*
   and *what data would I have to give it?* AI also shows up in three places
   at work: standalone chat assistants, AI features inside tools you already use
   (email, documents, spreadsheets, meetings), and tools your employer has
   approved. The skills today work in all three; the data rules differ.

## Key points

1. The model predicts plausible text. Plausible ≠ true.
2. It only knows what you tell it about your situation. Context is the biggest
   lever you have.
3. Precise-sounding numbers and names are the claims most worth checking.
4. Before handing over a task, ask: how bad if it's wrong, and what data goes
   in?

## Common problems and what to do

| Problem | Response |
| --- | --- |
| "So it's just autocomplete?" | "Yes, an extremely capable one. That's why what you give it matters so much" |
| Technical tangents (training, parameters, how it "thinks") | Park it: "Great question for the break." Out of scope; keep to what changes how you use it |
| "Will AI take my job?" | Acknowledge briefly; it's a real concern and out of scope today. Redirect: "Today is about which parts of your job it can help with" |
| A tool refuses or hedges heavily in Lab 01 step 3 | Good. Have that pair compare with a neighbour whose tool didn't |
| Someone's tool searches the web and gives real-looking citations | Ask: "Is that source about Dana's city? The case doesn't name one." |
