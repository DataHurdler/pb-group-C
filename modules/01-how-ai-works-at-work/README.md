# Module 01 — How AI Works, and Where It Fits at Work

Status: draft
Time: 9:20–10:20 (60 min — lecture 20 / activity 40)
Objective: LO1

## Objective

By the end of this module, participants will be able to **explain** why an AI
assistant produced a given output, in terms of prediction and context;
**flag** claims that are likely invented; and **sort** workplace tasks into
good fit / use with care / not for AI.

## Why this module is here

We assume most participants have used an AI assistant and had generic or
wrong answers back; the pre-check will show whether that holds for each
cohort. This module gives them a working mental model, just enough to predict what will help and where
the risks are, and a first sense of which work to hand to AI at all.
Everything after it follows: Module 02 because the model only knows what's in
the context, Modules 03–04 because "where it fits" is the business question.

### The four ideas

1. **Prediction, not lookup.** Language models are trained to predict the
   next word (strictly, the next token) given the text so far ([Anthropic, *Glossary*](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary);
   [OpenAI, 2023, *GPT-4 Technical Report*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)). The model isn't retrieving a stored answer. Its output also
   varies from run to run, so identical prompts can produce different answers
   ([Anthropic, *Glossary*](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)). Plausible isn't the same as true.
2. **Context is its working memory.** Anthropic's own glossary describes the
   context window as the model's "working memory", separate from its training
   data ([Anthropic, *Glossary*](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/glossary)). The model doesn't know your business, your customers, or
   your constraints unless they're in the conversation. Two caveats: some
   assistants can remember details across chats when memory is on
   ([OpenAI, 2024, *Memory and new controls for ChatGPT*](https://openai.com/index/memory-and-new-controls-for-chatgpt/)), and some can search the web. Lab 01 shows what a generic
   question gets back.
3. **Confident wrongness.** Models can produce plausible falsehoods, partly
   because standard training and evaluation reward guessing over admitting
   uncertainty ([Kalai et al., 2025, *Why Language Models Hallucinate*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)). In one study, 55% of GPT-3.5's citations and 18% of
   GPT-4's were fabricated ([Walters & Wilder, 2023, *Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5)). Tone tells you nothing about accuracy. Our
   rule of thumb: check numbers, names, quotes, and anything recent first.
   Models don't know events after their training data ends ([OpenAI, 2023, *GPT-4 Technical Report*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)). Tools
   that search the web help, but only if the sources are about *your*
   question.
4. **Where it fits at work.** The evidence is uneven, task by task. In a
   field experiment with BCG consultants, GPT-4 users completed 12.2% more
   tasks, 25.1% faster and with higher quality on tasks inside AI's
   capabilities, but were less likely to get a correct answer on a task
   outside them ([Dell'Acqua et al., 2023, *Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier*](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838)). On professional writing tasks, ChatGPT cut time by
   40% and raised quality by 18% ([Noy & Zhang, 2023, *Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586)). Calculations need checking: one
   model's accuracy at identifying prime numbers fell from 84% to 51% between
   two versions three months apart ([Chen, Zaharia & Zou, 2024, *How Is ChatGPT's Behavior Changing over Time?*](https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.5317da47)). Our rule of thumb is to ask two
   questions: *how bad is it if this is wrong?* and *what data would I have to
   give it?* AI also shows up in three places at work: standalone chat
   assistants, AI features inside office software ([Microsoft Support, *Microsoft Copilot help and learning*](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot); [Google, *AI Tools for Business*](https://workspace.google.com/solutions/ai/)), and tools
   your employer has approved. The skills today apply in all three; the data
   rules differ.

## Key points

1. The model predicts plausible text. Plausible ≠ true.
2. It only knows what you tell it about your situation. Context is the biggest
   lever you have.
3. Precise-sounding numbers and names are the claims most worth checking.
4. Before handing over a task, ask: how bad if it's wrong, and what data goes
   in?

## Common problems and what to do

| Problem | Response |
| --- | --- |
| "So it's just autocomplete?" | "Yes, an extremely capable one. That's why what you give it matters so much" |
| Technical tangents (training, parameters, how it "thinks") | Park it: "Great question for the break." Out of scope; keep to what changes how you use it |
| "Will AI take my job?" | Acknowledge briefly; it's a real concern and out of scope today. Redirect: "Today is about which parts of your job it can help with" |
| A tool refuses or hedges heavily in Lab 01 step 3 | Good. Have that pair compare with a neighbour whose tool didn't |
| Someone's tool searches the web and gives real-looking citations | Ask: "Is that source about Dana's city? The case doesn't name one." |
