# Lab 01 — Same Question, Different Answers

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Work in:** pairs
**Module:** 01 · **Objective:** LO1

## What you'll do

Ask an AI assistant the same business question several ways and watch what
changes. You'll catch it making something up, then sort everyday work tasks by
how far you'd trust AI with them.

## What you need

- Your AI chat assistant, open in a **new chat**. If you and your partner
  use different tools, even better
- [The Loop Laundry practice case](practice-case.md)
- Somewhere to take notes. [handouts/prompt-log.md](../handouts/prompt-log.md)
  works

## Steps

1. **Ask it cold (7 min).** Both partners, in new chats, type exactly:

   > Should a laundromat start a delivery subscription?

   Read both answers side by side, then regenerate one of them. What's the
   same, what's different, and how much of it would actually help Dana?

2. **Give it the case (8 min).** Start a new chat. Paste the whole practice
   case, then ask:

   > Based only on the information above, what are the three biggest risks of
   > Dana's subscription idea, and what should she find out first?

   Compare with step 1. List two specific things the answer now includes that it
   couldn't have known before.

3. **Catch a confident error (6 min).** In the same chat, ask:

   > What do the main competitors in Dana's city charge for laundry delivery,
   > and how big is the local market?

   Sort every factual claim in the answer into three columns:
   **From the case** · **Plausible, but where would it get that?** ·
   **Admits it doesn't know**. The case names no city and no competitors, so
   any specific figure is invented.

4. **Sort the work (7 min).** Without using the AI, put each task below in one
   column: **Good fit** · **Use with care** · **Not for AI**. Be ready to say
   why.

   | | |
   | --- | --- |
   | a. First draft of a job ad | g. Summarize a 20-page industry report you've read |
   | b. Decide which employee to promote | h. Calculate quarterly tax owed |
   | c. 20 name ideas for a new service | i. Reply to an angry customer |
   | d. Summarize a client's contract | j. Turn meeting notes into action items |
   | e. Check whether a supplier is legitimate | k. Predict next month's sales from 12 months of data |
   | f. Rewrite a policy in plain language | l. Explain a spreadsheet formula |

5. **Say why (2 min).** Together, finish this sentence: *"The answers changed
   because…"*

## You're done when

You have notes on the three comparisons, a sorted task list, and a
one-sentence explanation that mentions **context**.

## If you finish early

Ask the model: *"Which parts of your previous answer are you least confident
about, and why?"* Does its self-assessment match your column sort in step 3?

## Debrief questions

1. What did adding the case change, and what did it *not* fix?
2. Which task in step 4 did you and your partner disagree on?

---

### Instructor notes (remove from the participant copy)

- **Likely output** (re-run before each offering; models change). Step 1 is
  likely to give a generic pros/cons list. Step 2 should mention the $15k
  budget, no vehicle, no full-time hire, the 600 new units, and the
  cannibalization worry. Step 3 may produce invented price ranges and market
  sizes stated plainly. If it does, that's the moment to land the lesson.
- **Step 4 has no single answer key**, and that's the point. Defensible sorts:
  *good fit*: a, c, f, j, l; *use with care*: d (data sensitivity: is the
  contract confidential?), g (only if you can check it against the report), i
  (draft only; a human sends it), k (first-pass only; check against simple
  arithmetic); *not for AI* without other sources: b (a judgment about a person
  that you're accountable for), e (needs real verification), h (needs exact,
  current rules, so use the right tool or an accountant). The best debriefs come
  from disagreements over d, i, and k.
- **Where pairs get stuck.** Some tools refuse step 3 or hedge heavily. That's
  a good outcome. Ask that pair to compare with a neighbour whose tool *did*
  produce numbers. Some tools search the web and cite real sources. If so, ask:
  "Is that source about Dana's city? Dana's city isn't named."
- **While circulating,** listen for "it's wrong" vs. "it didn't have the
  information." The second is the insight you want.
